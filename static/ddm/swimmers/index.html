This web page is a divertissement. For fun, we took configurations close to those of our lab and used them to simulate some typical DDM experiments in your browser. The simulations are simple (for example, the cells do not interact) and rely on many approximations. Found something wrong or not working? Please let us know through the contact page.

Differential dynamic microscopy

Sizing particlesSwimming bacteria

How fast do bacteria swim?

Many bacteria swim, and how fast they swim and how many of them do it tells a microbiologist whether they are healthy, hungry or poisoned. Following cells one by one is slow and gives a few hundred tracks at best. Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) reads the swimming speeds and the share of swimmers from an ordinary microscope movie of thousands of cells in a few minutes, without following any of them (Wilson et al. 2011). Here a simulated microscope lets you run this motility assay yourself, from the raw movie to the distribution of swimming speeds, and see where it can go wrong.

Hands on

The bench

Change the bacteria, the microscope or the camera with the controls (on the left, or just below on a phone). The movie is recorded again and every step below updates. A run takes from a few seconds to about a minute, because every cell is imaged through a detailed model of the microscope's optics.

1

Set up the microscope

The microscope sees a round field 25 mm across in its image plane. A camera chip covers a rectangle in it, and you choose which part of the chip to read out: a smaller region can be read faster. A point-scanning confocal instead sweeps a laser spot over a square, line by line.

How one particle looks

The math: how each image is computed

Bright field. The condenser fills its aperture with mutually incoherent plane waves (spatial coherence) at the wavelengths of the lamp spectrum (temporal coherence). For each of them, the light scattered by the sphere, computed exactly with Mie theory from the particle and liquid refractive indices, interferes with the transmitted light after passing the objective pupil. The intensities are then added. The same formula covers the other transmitted-light methods by changing the illumination and the pupil: phase contrast lights the sample through a thin annulus and gives the objective pupil a ring that dims the direct light and shifts its phase by a quarter wave; dark field uses illumination steeper than the objective can collect, so only the |Es|2 term is left; DIC multiplies the pupil by i sin((κ·s + φ)/2), with s the shear and φ the bias, so the image depends on the direction of q. For a particle at distance z from focus, the Fourier transform of its image is

K(q, z) = ⟨ −(2π/k) P(κ)P(κ+q) S(θ) ei(kz(κ) − kz(κ+q))z / kz + c.c.(−q) ⟩source, λ + |Es|2 term

with S = (S1 + S2)/2 the Mie amplitude at the scattering angle θ and P the pupil with aplanatic apodization. At q = 0 the interference term equals minus the extinction cross-section (optical theorem), which the code checks.

Fluorescence. Emission is incoherent: the image is the dyed sphere convolved with the Debye point spread function (scalar, or vector at high NA). Confocal (spinning disk or point scanning): excitation spread times emission spread convolved with the pinhole projected on the sample; cross-talk between the disk pinholes is neglected.

Swimmers. A chosen share of the cells swims along its own axis at a speed drawn from a Schulz (gamma) distribution, keeps its Brownian motion, turns slowly by rotational diffusion and, if asked, tumbles at random times into a new direction. Swimmers bounce off the walls of the cell by mirroring the vertical part of their direction. Along the glass all cells lie flat and move only within the plane. The cells do not interact and are not slowed down near the glass.

Movie. Dilute particles diffuse in 3D (Stokes-Einstein, reflecting walls); each frame is the sum of their images, recorded with the detector's pixel size, quantum efficiency, shot noise, read noise and full well. Each particle is imaged when its row is exposed (rolling shutters) or scanned (point scanners). Included: spherical aberration from focusing into the liquid; optionally vector (high-NA) optics, settling under gravity and slower translation near the glass. Neglected: multiple scattering, interference between light scattered by different particles, other aberrations (for example a wrong cover-glass thickness), polarizers acting on rods and ellipsoids beyond the paraxial approximation, imperfect DIC prisms, stray light in dark field other than a fixed share of the illumination, slower rotation near the glass.

Particles that turn. Each particle carries an axis u that diffuses on the sphere (rotational diffusion DR), and moves with D∥ along it and D⊥ across it in its own frame (Perrin's formulas for ellipsoids, Tirado and Garcia de la Torre for rods). Its polarizability is a tensor α = α⊥ I + (α∥ − α⊥) u u, from the material's birefringence and from the shape. With a polarizer along eP and an analyzer along eA, the interference term is weighted by (eA·eP)(eA·α·eP) and the scattered intensity by |eA·α·eP|2. Spheres keep their exact Mie field, scaled by these weights. Rods and ellipsoids are filled with small scatterers (Rayleigh-Gans), which also gives their shape in the image; in the scattered intensity the shape sets a tilt-dependent brightness ⟨|F(Q)|2⟩ over the illumination and collection angles.

Checked against miepython (amplitudes to 1e-10), the optical theorem, a brute-force sum over 3000 source points, and the amplitude formula of fastDDM tutorial 6 in its range of validity.

2

Record a movie

In fastDDM

The analysis below uses the same steps as fastDDM. With your own movie you would start from the image files:

import fastddm as fd
img_seq = fd.read_images(file_names)
Frames in bursts: long movies from few frames

A camera can also be triggered at chosen times. With "Frame timing: in bursts" the page takes the frames in bursts of B frames at the frame interval δt, bursts of bursts (B s) δt apart, and so on: frame k, written in base B with digits d0, d1, ..., is taken at

tk = δt [d0 + d1 (B s) + d2 (B s)2 + ...]

With s = 1 the frames are evenly spaced; with 256 frames and B = 4 the movie lasts about 7, 22 or 50 times longer for s = 2, 3 or 4. As in a multi-tau correlator, the short delays come from pairs inside a burst and the long ones from pairs in distant bursts. The price is gaps: inside a burst the delays go up to (B − 1) δt, the next ones start near (B s − B + 1) δt, so each level leaves out a factor of about s. The structure function is then computed from pairs of frames grouped by how far apart they are (one group per frame interval up to 10, then 12 groups per factor of ten), with up to as many pairs per group as there are frames. The movie in step 2 is played one frame after the other, so the jumps between bursts show as sudden changes.

3

Subtract two frames

Subtract a frame from one taken a little later. Everything that stays still, such as dust or uneven light, cancels out. What is left is what moved, plus the camera noise.

Move the delay slider. With a short delay the difference is faint. With a longer one it grows, until the two frames have nothing left in common.

The math: the difference image
ΔI(x; t, Δt) = I(x, t + Δt) − I(x, t)

Anything that does not change in time disappears from ΔI. This is why DDM tolerates dirty optics and uneven illumination.

4

Split it into stripe patterns

Any image can be built by adding up stripe patterns of different spacing and direction. The Fourier transform tells how much of each pattern the difference image contains, averaged over the whole movie.

The centre of the map holds wide stripes, the edge holds fine ones. Click the map to see which pattern a point stands for. The map uses the same delay as step 3.

Distance from the centre is the size of the wave vector, q = 2π / spacing. The particles have no preferred direction, so the map is round (DIC and drift are exceptions), and we average it along circles to get one value for each q. The dashed circle marks the finest pattern the objective can resolve.

The math: image structure function
d(q, Δt) = ⟨ |F[ΔI](q; t, Δt)|2 ⟩t

Here F is the two-dimensional Fourier transform and the average runs over all start times t. For motion with no preferred direction, d depends only on q = |q|, and the azimuthal average gives d(q, Δt). The cross qx = 0 or qy = 0 is left out because image edges add artefacts there.

dqt = fd.ddm(img_seq, range(1, len(img_seq) // 2))
dqt.pixel_size = pixel_size        # um
dqt.set_frame_rate(frame_rate)     # frames per second
ccm = fd.mask.central_cross_mask(dqt.shape[1:])
aa = fd.azimuthal_average(dqt, bins=len(dqt.ky) // 2,
                          range=(0, dqt.ky[-1]), mask=ccm)
5

Watch each pattern change

For each stripe spacing, ask how different the movie looks after a given delay. Fine stripes (red) change quickly, because a cell only has to move a little to shift them. Wide stripes (blue) take longer. Each curve rises in two steps: first quickly, because the swimmers carry the pattern away, then slowly, as the cells that only jiggle wander off.

Each curve rises from 0, nothing has changed, to 1, the pattern is completely renewed. The points are the measurement, the lines a fit.

Each curve is the structure function d(q, Δt) for one q. It rises from the camera noise level B(q) to A(q) + B(q). The lines are one swimmer model fitted to all q at once, with a shared speed distribution, share of swimmers and diffusion coefficient. The "Change" view removes A and B so all curves share one scale.

Predict first: the bacteria swim twice as fast. Do the curves move to the left or to the right?
To the left, but only the first, fast step: twice the speed renews each pattern in half the time. The slow step of the cells that do not swim stays where it was. The bench now runs with the faster bacteria so you can check.
The math: the swimmer model
d(q, Δt) = A(q) [1 − f(q, Δt)] + B(q)

For independent cells f is the average of e−iq·Δr over all cells. A share α swims in straight lines, in random directions, at speeds v from a Schulz distribution of mean v̄ and relative spread σ (shape Z = 1/σ2 − 1); all cells also diffuse with D (Wilson et al. 2011):

f = e−Dq2Δt [ (1 − α) + α ⟨sin(qvΔt) / (qvΔt)⟩v ]
⟨sin(qvΔt) / (qvΔt)⟩v = (Z + 1) sin(Z arctan Λ) / [Z qv̄Δt (1 + Λ2)Z/2],   Λ = qv̄Δt / (Z + 1)

The sine over its argument is the average of e−iq·vΔt over directions in 3D. For cells swimming in the image plane the average over directions in the plane is the Bessel function J0(qvΔt) instead. The page fits α, v̄, σ and D to all q at once; A and B of each q follow by linear least squares. Several starting points are tried and the best fit is kept.

Two safeguards. When the cells that do not swim barely move during the movie, a smaller α with a larger A describes the curves just as well (cells that stay put add nothing to the changes from frame to frame). So the page also tries D fixed at the value expected from the size and shape of the cells and the viscosity of the liquid, and keeps it when the data do not ask for another one (cost at most 3 % higher); for rods it may add the fading of the still cells as they turn, a share b at rate 6DR. And it fits cells that do not swim at all (α = 0, with b and DR free): if that is about as good (cost at most 10 % higher), it reports no swimming, because turning rods alone can pass for slow swimmers.

The depth of field. Wilson's model counts only sideways motion. A cell at depth z adds K(q, z) e−iq·x to the image, with K the optical kernel of step 1, so a swimmer that also moves up or down fades from each pattern. With Cq(δz) the overlap of K(q, z) and K(q, z + δz) averaged over the cell, a swimmer that has moved s = vΔt gives

hq(s) = ∫01 dμ J0(qs √(1 − μ2)) Re Cq(sμ)

in place of the sine over its argument (they are equal when C = 1), averaged over the speeds; the cells that do not swim get Cq averaged over their axial diffusion. This model is used automatically in the confocal modes. As far as we know it has not been published.

The stroke. Algae and sperm do not glide: each beat moves the cell, or the sperm head, back and forth about its mean path. Martinez et al. (2012) wrote the displacement as vΔt + A[sin(2πfΔt + φ) − sin φ], with a random phase φ for each cell. Averaged over φ and over the speeds, the sine over its argument of Wilson's model becomes

∫01 dμ ⟨cos(qμvΔt)⟩v J0(2qμA |sin(πfΔt)|)

with μ the cosine between the path and q. The curves dip and rise again once per beat, so the camera needs at least two frames per beat. For a stroke across the path (the sperm head) in a random direction about it, the Bessel factor becomes J0(qA |sin(πfΔt)| √(1 − μ2))2. The page fits A and f with the other parameters, after a search over a grid of beat frequencies.

B_est, _ = fd.noise_est.estimate_camera_noise(aa, mode='polyfit', num_points=5)
A_est = 2 * aa.var - B_est
# swimmer model: write it as an lmfit model and fit all q together, as in
# fastDDM tutorial 8a (there with a compressed exponential at each q)
6

Who swims, who only diffuses, and how fast

Each curve of step 5 falls in two steps when only some cells swim. Two ways of plotting the same curves tell the two kinds of motion apart. A swimmer at constant speed covers a distance in a time proportional to it, so a pattern renews when qΔt reaches a fixed value: against qΔt (first chart) the fast first steps of all patterns fall on one curve. A cell that only diffuses needs four times as long to go twice as far, so against q2Δt (second chart) the slow last steps fall on one curve instead. The level between the two steps is the share of cells that do not swim.

Now plot how fast each pattern changes against how fine it is. A swimmer covers twice the distance in twice the time, so halving the stripe spacing makes the change twice as fast: on these logarithmic axes the points fall on a line of slope 1. Cells that only jiggle need four times as long to wander twice as far, which gives slope 2.

The speed itself comes from fitting one swimmer model to all the curves of step 5 at once. It gives the mean swimming speed, how much the speeds spread, the share of cells that swim and how fast the others diffuse.

Hollow grey points are outside what this movie can measure: patterns as large as the image or finer than the microscope resolves, changes faster than one frame or too slow for the movie, signal lost in the camera noise. They are left out of the result.

Predict first: half of the swimmers stop swimming. Does the measured mean speed change?
No. The fast step of each curve gets smaller, so the share of swimmers drops, but the swimmers that are left are as fast as before. The bench now runs with half as many swimmers.
The math: rates, v₀ and the distribution of speeds

The points of the plot come from fitting each q on its own with a compressed exponential, f = exp[−(ΓΔt)β], as fastDDM tutorial 8a does for swimming bacteria (Lu et al. 2012). For swimmers Γ = v₀ q. This v₀ is a speed scale, not the mean speed: in 3D the sine over its argument falls to 1/e at qvΔt ≈ 2.2, the compressed exponential at qv₀Δt = 1, so v̄ ≈ 2.2 v₀ for a narrow distribution of speeds and 2.4-2.8 v₀ for broad ones (in the plane 1.7-2.0 v₀). A compressed exponent β ≈ 1.35 corresponds to a broad distribution, with a relative spread of about 70 %.

The distribution of speeds can be found without a model: for swimmers in 3D f(x) = ∫ dv P(v) sin(xv)/(xv) with x = qΔt, which inverts to P(v) = (2v/π) ∫ dx x f(x) sin(xv) (Lattuada et al. 2025, Eq. 58). Applied to the measured curves the integral is noisy, so the page instead finds non-negative weights on a grid of speeds (with one weight at zero speed for the cells that do not swim) that reproduce the curves of all q together, with a small penalty on sharp wiggles.

About this tool

    
This page is a simple tool, generated with Claude Opus 5.5, that simulates a realistic motility assay in which DDM measures how fast bacteria swim. The microscope, light sources, cameras, spinning disk and point scanner are modelled mostly on the instruments of our SoMeX lab. The cells do not interact with each other: each one swims or diffuses on its own, so every sample is a dilute suspension. The aim is not to produce accurate, research-quality data, but to give anyone thinking of using DDM an idea of the many options and of how each one affects a DDM experiment.

    
If you find something that looks wrong or does not work, please let us know through the contact page.
