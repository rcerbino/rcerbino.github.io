Is it a liquid or a solid? Microrheology with tracer beads

Many soft materials are a bit of both: a polymer solution springs back if you poke it quickly but flows if you wait, and a gel that is just forming is barely solid. Microrheology measures it without shearing the sample: it mixes in a few tiny beads and watches how far the thermal kicks of the molecules push them in a given time. In a liquid the beads wander off; in a solid they rattle in place. From that motion follow the storage modulus G' (how elastic) and the loss modulus G'' (how viscous), over many decades of frequency, from a drop of sample. Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) reads the motion of the beads from an ordinary microscope movie, without following any of them (Edera et al. 2017). Here a simulated microscope lets you do it on materials whose moduli are known exactly.

Hands on

The bench

Choose the material and the beads, then the microscope and the camera (controls on the left, or just below on a phone). The movie is recorded again and every step below updates. "Copy a link to this setup" gives an address that opens the bench with the same settings.

1

Set up the microscope

The microscope sees a round field 25 mm across in its image plane. A camera chip covers a rectangle in it, and you choose which part of the chip to read out. For microrheology a fast camera matters: the beads move only a little, and the fastest relaxations of the material are over in milliseconds. A small region of interest reads out faster.

How one bead looks

The math: how each image is computed, and how the beads move

As on the sizing page: the light scattered by each bead (Mie theory) interferes with the transmitted light through the condenser and objective apertures, or the dyed bead is convolved with the point spread function (fluorescence and confocal). Each frame is the sum of the images of all beads at their positions, with the detector's pixel size, quantum efficiency, shot noise, read noise and full well. The material itself does not show.

Motion. The material is a solvent of viscosity ηs, Maxwell modes (modulus Gk, relaxation time τk) and, for a solid, an equilibrium modulus G∞:

G'(ω) = G∞ + Σ Gk (ωτk)2 / [1 + (ωτk)2],    G''(ω) = ηsω + Σ Gk ωτk / [1 + (ωτk)2]

A bead of radius a feels each mode as a spring 6πaGk in series with a dashpot 6πaGkτk, the plateau as a spring to its resting place and the solvent as a dashpot, each dashpot with its thermal noise. This is a linear (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) process, simulated exactly at every frame, along x, y and z alike; its mean squared displacement obeys the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation exactly. The beads do not interact, and the material is the same everywhere: no mesh that small beads slip through, no beads that stick.

2

Record a movie

The frames are log-spaced: in each cycle the first ones follow at the frame rate, then the gaps grow, and the cycle repeats. The same 512 frames then hold delays from milliseconds to about a minute, many decades of frequency in one movie.

In fastDDM

The structure function below is the same as fastDDM's. With your own movie you would start from the image files:

import fastddm as fd
img_seq = fd.read_images(file_names)
3

Subtract two frames

Subtract a frame from one taken a little later. Everything that stays still cancels out; what is left is what moved, plus the camera noise.

The math: the difference image
ΔI(x; t, Δt) = I(x, t + Δt) − I(x, t)
4

Split it into stripe patterns

Any image can be built by adding up stripe patterns of different spacing and direction. The Fourier transform tells how much of each pattern the difference image contains, averaged over the whole movie. The centre of the map holds wide stripes, the edge fine ones. The beads move the same way in every direction, so the map is round.

A bead that moves by a fraction of a stripe spacing already changes that pattern; to renew a wide pattern it must go much further. So fine stripes are a magnifying glass for small motions, wide stripes for large ones, and together they cover displacements from nanometres to micrometres.

The second map shows how much of each pattern is left after the delay: red where it is still all there, the colour of the page where it has faded. In a gel the wide patterns stay red at every delay.

The dashed circle on the strength map marks the finest pattern the objective can resolve.

The math: structure function of tracer beads

For beads that do not interact, with Gaussian displacements,

d(q, Δt) = ⟨ |F[ΔI](q; t, Δt)|2 ⟩ = A(q) [1 − f(q, Δt)] + B,    f(q, Δt) = exp[−q2 ⟨Δr2(Δt)⟩ / 4]

with ⟨Δr2⟩ the mean squared displacement in the image plane. For free diffusion ⟨Δr2⟩ = 4DΔt and f = e−Dq2Δt, as on the sizing page. Map axes: qx to the right, qy up.

dqt = fd.ddm(img_seq, lags)      # lags: the log-spaced delays
dqt.pixel_size = pixel_size      # um
aa = fd.azimuthal_average(dqt, bins=bins, range=(0, dqt.ky[-1]))
5

Watch each pattern change

For each stripe spacing, follow how much of the pattern is left as the delay grows. Fine stripes (red) change first, because a bead only has to move a little to shift them; wide ones (blue) change later. In a liquid every curve falls to zero in the end. In a gel the beads cannot leave their cages, so the curves level off well above zero: they never forget their pattern, and the wider the stripes the higher the level.

The points are f(q, Δt) = 1 − (d − B) / A. The lines are exp[−q2 ⟨Δr2⟩ / 4] with the mean squared displacement of step 6 (leaving out the small effect of the depth of field), the same for every q: if the beads' displacements are Gaussian, one displacement explains all the curves.

Predict first: in a gel the beads cannot leave their cages. Do the curves still fall to zero at long delays?
No. Each bead only rattles in its cage, so part of every pattern never changes: the curves level off above zero, higher for wider stripes, which a small rattle hardly moves. The height of the plateau gives how far the beads rattle, and from it the elastic modulus of the gel. The bench now runs with a soft gel.
6

From the curves to the moduli

Each curve of step 5 gives, at each delay, how far the beads have moved on average: the mean squared displacement. Fine stripes give the small displacements of short delays, wide stripes the large ones of long delays, and where they overlap they agree. Free diffusion makes the displacement grow in proportion to the delay (a slope of 1 on these axes); beads held in cages make it level off (a slope of 0).

A rule that goes back to Einstein turns the displacement into the moduli: the stiffer the material, the less the beads move, and the faster it relaxes, the sooner they escape. A delay Δt stands for a frequency of about 1/Δt.

The generalised Stokes-Einstein relation turns the displacement into the moduli: the stiffer the material, the less the beads move, and the faster it relaxes, the sooner they escape. A delay Δt stands for a frequency of about 1/Δt.

Predict first: the beads are twice as large. Do the moduli change?
No. Larger beads move less: their mean squared displacement halves. But the rule that turns displacement into moduli multiplies it by the size again, so the moduli, which belong to the material, stay the same. The bench now runs with beads twice as large (at most 3 µm).
The math: from f to the moduli
⟨Δr2(Δt)⟩ = −(4 / q2) ln f(q, Δt),    J(t) = πa ⟨Δr2(t)⟩3D / kBT,    ⟨Δr2⟩3D = 1.5 ⟨Δr2⟩

Amplitude and noise. A(q) = 2⟨|F(q)|2⟩ − B, from the mean power of each pattern, which holds the whole amplitude even when part of the pattern never changes during the movie; B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise2), the camera noise, the same at every q. Displacement. At each q and delay, ⟨Δr2⟩ is the value that gives the measured f, with the depth of field: beads moving up or down change their image too, which adds a factor Cq(⟨Δz2⟩) through the optics of step 1, with ⟨Δz2⟩ = ⟨Δr2⟩/2. Only points where f is clearly below 1 (by three times its noise, and by at least 1 %) and above 0.15 are used, from the fourth q bin up; the q are combined with weights from the noise of ln ⟨Δr2⟩, at least three per delay. The noise of f and the uncertainty of the result come from a jackknife over ten parts of the movie.

Moduli. G*(ω) = 1 / [iω Ĵ(ω)]. The measured J(t) is fitted with springs and dashpots side by side (Kelvin-Voigt elements, retardation times four per decade) and a free flow t/η, all with positive weights; the moduli of that sum follow exactly. A direct transform of the measured points (Evans et al. 2009) rang with their noise. For comparison (Researcher level) Mason's local power-law formulas, |G*(ω)| = kBT / [πa⟨Δr2(1/ω)⟩3D Γ(1 + α)], G' = |G*| cos(πα/2), G'' = |G*| sin(πα/2), with α the local slope of ⟨Δr2⟩ on logarithmic axes.

What this page adds to the published methods

    
DDM microrheology was introduced by Edera et al. (2017), and Bayles, Squires and Helgeson (2017) did the same independently: f(q, Δt) turned into a mean squared displacement at each q, then into the moduli through the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation. Edera et al. chose A and B by making the displacements from all q agree; in a simulation the camera is known, so this page gets B from the photon count and A from the mean power instead. The steps below are not in the papers we cite: we added them while testing the page against its own simulations, and some may exist elsewhere. At the "Addict" level each can be switched off, and the result table shows a plain method on the same movie (A from the variance, B from the first delays, no depth of field, q where the displacement is below 1/q², Mason's formulas).

    
    
      
  • Amplitude from the mean power. A = 2⟨|F(q)|²⟩ − B instead of 2 var − B. Step 6. When the widest patterns are not renewed within the movie the variance misses part of A: on the standard micelles this alone reads the viscosity three times too low (85 instead of 250 mPa s).
    • 
      
  • Camera noise known from the detector. B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise²), the same at every q. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Depth of field. Beads moving up and down change each pattern too; the inversion includes it through the optics of step 1, as on the sizing page. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Points chosen by their noise. Each q is used where f is clearly below 1 and above 0.15, weighted by the noise of ln ⟨Δr²⟩ from a jackknife over ten parts of the movie; the widest patterns are left out. Step 6. The widest patterns read the displacement up to twice too large in our tests.
    • 
      
  • Moduli from a fitted retardation spectrum, positive by construction. Step 6. On the soft micelles Mason's formulas read G' near the crossover about 20 % off; the spectrum stays within a few percent.

About this tool

    
This page is a simple tool, generated with Claude Opus 5.5, that simulates a realistic microscopy experiment in which DDM is used to measure the moduli of a soft material from the motion of tracer beads. The microscope, light sources, cameras, spinning disk and point scanner are modelled mostly on the instruments of our SoMeX lab. The beads are spheres that do not interact with each other, in a material that is the same everywhere and whose moduli are set exactly; the concentrated micelles come from the measurements cited on the page, the other materials have values chosen for the page. The aim is not to produce research-quality data, but to give anyone thinking of using DDM an idea of what microrheology can measure, and of what limits it.

    
If you find something that looks wrong or does not work, please let us know through the contact page.
