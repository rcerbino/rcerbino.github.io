Numbers you may need. Boltzmann constant kB = 1.381 × 10-23 J/K, so kBT = 4.07 × 10-21 J at 22 °C. Viscosity of water: 0.955 mPa s at 22 °C, 0.596 mPa s at 45 °C. Avogadro's number NA = 6.022 × 1023 per mole. 1 µm2/s = 10-12 m2/s. Stokes-Einstein relation: D = kBT / (6πηR) for a sphere of radius R in a liquid of viscosity η. A pattern of wave vector q fades by diffusion at the rate Γ = Dq2, in a time of about 1/Γ.

The method

For every page. The landing page shows the steps.

  1. The stripes of a pattern in the image are 3 µm apart. What is its wave vector q? And the stripe spacing of q = 10 µm-1?Compute

    Model answer

    q = 2π / spacing = 2π / 3 µm = 2.1 µm-1. The other way round, a spacing of 2π / q = 0.63 µm for q = 10 µm-1. Larger q means finer stripes.

  2. A speck of dust sits on the camera window and the light is brighter in one corner of the image. What cancels when two frames are subtracted, and what can still bias the analysis?Discuss

    Model answer

    DDM works on differences of frames. What stays the same from one frame to the next, the speck and the uneven light included, cancels in each difference; only what moved is left. (A speck that moves, or light that flickers, would not cancel.) Two limits: the uneven light also multiplies the image of each particle, so particles in the bright corner weigh more in A(q) than those in the dim one; and the static background still adds to the mean power of each frame, so a page that takes A from that power (as Crowded particles and Liquid or solid do) must leave it out first.

    Assumes: a background that adds to the image and does not change in time.

  3. The structure function is d(q, Δt) = A(q) [1 - f(q, Δt)] + B(q). What are d at a very short delay and at a very long one? What does each tell you?Discuss

    Model answer

    At a very short delay nothing has moved yet: f = 1 and d = B, the camera noise of two independent frames (a frame subtracted from itself, at exactly zero delay, would give 0). At a very long delay, if every pattern has been renewed, f = 0 and d = A + B; if something never relaxes, as in a gel or a caged sheet, f stops at a plateau and d stays below A + B. So B is read at the start of the curve, A as the height of the rise, and f, which goes from 1 to 0, says how much of each pattern is still there after Δt. How f falls with Δt holds the physics: diffusion, swimming, drift, or a cage that stops the fall.

    Assumes: camera noise independent from frame to frame; f = 0 only when the movie is long enough for the sample to renew completely.

  4. The fit of the curves looks perfect. Can the result still be wrong?Discuss

    Model answer

    Yes. The fit only says that the model describes how the patterns fade. Turning the rates into a size, a speed or a modulus needs inputs the movie does not give: the viscosity and the temperature, the pixel size, the magnification, the particle radius. A wrong input gives a wrong result from the same good fit (exercise 1.4).

Sizing particles

Page: How big are they?

  1. Beads of 200 nm in diameter are in water at 22 °C. What is their diffusion coefficient? How long does a pattern of q = 1 µm-1 take to fade, and one of q = 5 µm-1?Compute

    Model answer

    D = kBT / (3πηd) = 4.07 × 10-21 / (3π × 0.955 × 10-3 × 200 × 10-9) = 2.26 × 10-12 m2/s = 2.26 µm2/s.

    1/(Dq2) = 0.44 s at q = 1 µm-1, and 25 times less, 18 ms, at q = 5 µm-1: a pattern five times finer fades 25 times faster.

    Assumes: dilute spheres (no neighbours), far from walls, Stokes-Einstein drag, simple exponential decay.

  2. The beads become twice as large. By what factor does the fading time of a given pattern change? Which pattern now fades in the time the old one took?Predict

    Model answer

    D halves, so every pattern takes twice as long to fade: the curves move to the right. Since 1/(Dq2) is fixed, q2 has to double: the pattern with √2 = 1.41 times larger q (finer stripes) now fades as fast as the old one did.

    Try it on the page: 400 nm beads.

  3. The movie has 128 × 128 pixels of 0.325 µm, 100 frames per second and 256 frames. Which wave vectors does the image hold? A pattern is measured well if its rate Γ lies between about 2 divided by the longest delay (half the movie) and 1 divided by the shortest. Which q can measure the 200 nm beads of 1.1?Compute

    Model answer

    The widest pattern spans the image: q = 2π / (128 × 0.325 µm) = 0.15 µm-1. The finest has two pixels per stripe: q = π / 0.325 µm = 9.7 µm-1.

    The longest delay is 1.28 s and the shortest 0.01 s, so Γ must lie between 1.6 s-1 and 100 s-1. With D = 2.26 µm2/s: q from √(1.6/2.26) = 0.83 to √(100/2.26) = 6.6 µm-1. A faster camera extends the range to finer patterns, a longer movie to wider ones (the card "A movie that is too short" on the page).

    Assumes: evenly spaced frames, the longest useful delay half the movie, and a simple exponential decay.

  4. The beads are in a mix of half water and half glycerol (viscosity about 5.6 mPa s at 22 °C), but the analysis is told the liquid is water. Does the fit look worse? What size comes out?Predict

    Model answer

    The fit looks just as good: the movie only gives D, and D is measured as well as before. The size is wrong. The analysis divides kBT by the viscosity it was told, 0.955 mPa s, instead of the true 5.6 mPa s, so the size comes out 5.6 / 0.955 = 5.9 times too large: about 1.2 µm for 200 nm beads.

  5. You warm the water from 22 to 45 °C. How much faster do the patterns fade? Does the size change?Compute

    Model answer

    D is proportional to T/η: (318.15 / 295.15) × (0.955 / 0.596) = 1.08 × 1.60 = 1.73. The patterns fade 1.7 times faster, mostly because the water is less viscous. The size does not change, as long as the analysis uses the new temperature and viscosity.

    Assumes: the same beads, no change of the liquid other than its viscosity, the analysis told the new temperature.

Swimming cells

Page: How fast do they swim?

  1. Bacteria swim at 20 µm/s; those that do not swim diffuse with D = 0.3 µm2/s. For a pattern of q = 1 µm-1, compare the time a swimmer needs to renew it, about 1/(qv), with the time diffusion needs, 1/(Dq2). What do the curves look like?Compute

    Model answer

    1/(qv) = 1/(1 × 20) = 0.05 s for the swimmers; 1/(Dq2) = 3.3 s for the cells that only diffuse, 67 times longer. Each curve rises in two steps: a fast one, from the swimmers, and a slow one, from the rest. The height of the first step against the whole rise gives the share of swimmers.

    Assumes: straight swimming at one speed (a spread of speeds blurs the first step), diffusion of the non-swimmers only.

  2. The rate of swimming grows as q, that of diffusion as q2. At which q would the two be equal, for the cells of 2.1? Is that pattern in the image of a 10× objective with 0.65 µm pixels?Compute

    Model answer

    qv = Dq2 gives q = v/D = 20 / 0.3 = 67 µm-1, stripes 0.09 µm apart. The finest pattern of the image is π / 0.65 = 4.8 µm-1: at every q of the movie, swimming renews the patterns far faster than diffusion, which is why the two steps stay well apart.

    Assumes: the rates as stated, one swimming speed, no interactions between cells.

  3. The bacteria swim twice as fast. Do the curves move to the left or to the right?Predict

    Model answer

    To the left, but only the first, fast step: twice the speed renews each pattern in half the time. The slow step of the cells that do not swim stays where it was.

  4. Half of the swimmers, picked at random, stop. What happens to the share of swimmers and to the mean speed that the page measures?Predict

    Model answer

    The share halves: the fast step of each curve is half as high. The mean speed stays the same, because the cells that still swim are a random half of the swimmers, as fast as before.

    Try it on the page: 30 % swimmers instead of 60 %.

Drifting particles

Page: Where are they going?

  1. Polystyrene (1.05 g/mL) beads settle in water at the speed v = (2/9) Δρ g a2 / η, with a their radius. How fast do beads of 200 nm and of 2 µm in diameter settle at 22 °C?Compute

    Model answer

    Δρ = 50 kg/m3. For a = 100 nm: v = (2/9) × 50 × 9.81 × (10-7)2 / 0.955 × 10-3 = 1.1 × 10-9 m/s, 1.1 nm/s. Ten times the radius gives a hundred times the speed: 0.11 µm/s for a = 1 µm.

    Assumes: Stokes drag on a sphere far from walls, densities 1.05 and 1.00 g/mL, water at 22 °C.

  2. A drift shows in a pattern when it shifts the pattern noticeably before diffusion fades it: qv > Dq2, so q < v/D. For the 200 nm beads of 3.1, how wide would that pattern be? Can a movie show it?Compute

    Model answer

    v/D = 1.1 × 10-3 µm/s / 2.26 µm2/s = 4.9 × 10-4 µm-1: stripes 2π/q = 13 mm apart, far wider than any microscope image. In one pattern the drift is lost under diffusion. The page combines all patterns and all delays, so it does better than this rule, but for these beads it still only reports the slowest drift the movie could show (its "predict first" question).

    Assumes: one pattern at a time, the single-pattern rule qv > Dq2; the page does better by combining patterns and delays.

  3. A plug flow carries the particles at 10 µm/s along x. For a pattern of q = 1 µm-1 along x, how fast does its phase turn, and how long is one full swing? And for the same q along y?Compute

    Model answer

    The pattern moves with the flow, and its phase turns by qvΔt = 10 rad per second: one full swing (2π) in 0.63 s. Along y the flow does not move the stripes across themselves: no swing, only the fading by diffusion.

    Assumes: a uniform plug flow and no velocity gradient across the depth in focus.

  4. The flow is reversed. Does the strength map change? And the phase map?Predict

    Model answer

    The strength map does not: it keeps how far each pattern travelled, not which way. The phase map turns its colours around, because each phase now turns the other way.

    Try it on the page: the flow reversed.

Liquid or solid

Page: Liquid or solid? On it, f(q, Δt) = exp(-q2 <Δr2> / 4), with <Δr2> the mean squared displacement in the image plane, and the creep compliance is J = πa × 1.5 <Δr2> / (kBT) for beads of radius a.

  1. Beads of 0.5 µm in diameter are in water at 22 °C. What is their mean squared displacement in the image plane after 1 s? What slope does it have against the delay, on log scales?Compute

    Model answer

    D = kBT / (6πηa) = 4.07 × 10-21 / (6π × 0.955 × 10-3 × 0.25 × 10-6) = 0.91 µm2/s. In two dimensions <Δr2> = 4DΔt = 3.6 µm2 after 1 s. Slope 1: a simple liquid.

    Assumes: a purely viscous liquid, spheres far from walls, motion in the image plane only.

  2. The sample is a Maxwell fluid with a modulus of 0.5 Pa and a relaxation time of 0.5 s, like the wormlike micelles of the page. At which angular frequency do G' and G'' cross? What is its viscosity at long times, and how long must the movie last to see it flow?Compute

    Model answer

    They cross at ω = 1/τ = 2 rad/s (0.32 Hz). The viscosity is G0τ = 0.25 Pa s, 260 times that of water. The beads only diffuse freely after the relaxation time: the movie must last several times 0.5 s, a few seconds at least, to show the flow; a shorter one sees an elastic solid.

    Assumes: a single Maxwell mode, linear response, beads much larger than the structure of the fluid.

  3. In a soft gel the curves of 0.5 µm beads level off: at q = 2 µm-1, f stays at 0.8. How far do the beads rattle, and what is the elastic modulus of the gel?Compute

    Model answer

    0.8 = exp(-q2<Δr2>/4) gives <Δr2> = -4 ln(0.8) / q2 = 0.22 µm2 in the image plane, a rattle of about 0.33 µm along each axis.

    Then G = 1/J = kBT / (πa × 1.5 <Δr2>) = 4.07 × 10-21 / (π × 0.25 × 10-6 × 1.5 × 0.22 × 10-12) = 0.016 Pa: a very soft gel.

    Assumes: a Gaussian plateau, an elastic plateau in the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation, beads much larger than the mesh, no slip.

  4. The beads are twice as large. Do the moduli change?Predict

    Model answer

    No. Larger beads move less: their mean squared displacement halves. But the compliance multiplies it by the radius, so G' and G'', which belong to the material, stay the same, as long as the beads are much larger than the mesh of the material.

    Try it on the page: 1 µm beads.

Crowded particles

Page: How do they arrange in a crowd? The particles are hard spheres 1 µm across, at 30 % of the volume.

  1. The structure factor S(q) peaks at q = 6.3 µm-1. What distance does that pattern correspond to? Compare it with the size of the particles.Compute

    Model answer

    2π/q = 1.0 µm: about one diameter. The peak marks the spacing of nearest neighbours, which in a dense crowd sit nearly in contact.

    Assumes: the peak of S(q) at about 2π over the nearest-neighbour distance, as for a dense liquid.

  2. Without hydrodynamic interactions, collective diffusion at short times is D(q) = D0/S(q). The page finds S = 1.46 at the peak and S = 0.15 at the widest pattern. How fast does each fade, against one particle alone?Compute

    Model answer

    At the peak D/D0 = 1/1.46 = 0.68: the pattern at the spacing of neighbours, which the crowd favours, fades slowly (de Gennes narrowing). At the widest pattern D/D0 = 1/0.15 = 6.7: a crowd is hard to compress, so a ripple in its density evens out fast.

    Assumes: short times, no hydrodynamic interactions (with them, D(q) = D0H(q)/S(q)).

  3. For hard spheres, S at q → 0 is S(0) = (1 - φ)4 / [(1 + 2φ)2 - φ3(4 - φ)] (Carnahan-Starling). Compute it at φ = 0.30 and 0.45.Compute

    Model answer

    φ = 0.30: 0.2401 / 2.460 = 0.098. φ = 0.45: 0.0915 / 3.287 = 0.028. S(0), the reduced compressibility, falls about 3.5 times. The bulk modulus falls with S(0) and grows with the number density: S(0) = ρkBTκT, so the modulus 1/κT = ρkBT/S(0) grows by 1.5 × 3.5 = 5.3 from 30 % to 45 % (the density grows 1.5 times). The page's widest pattern, S = 0.15 at 30 %, sits above S(0) because q there is not quite zero.

    Assumes: hard spheres of fixed size, at the same temperature; the Carnahan-Starling equation of state.

  4. Same crowd, but only one particle in twenty is dyed. What peak height do the dyed particles show?Predict

    Model answer

    The measured S(q) is 1 + x [S(q) - 1], with x = 0.05 the dyed fraction: 1 + 0.05 × 0.46 = 1.02. The peak all but disappears, although the crowd is as ordered as before: pairs of dyed neighbours are too rare. The patterns now follow single particles held back by their cages.

    Try it on the page: one particle in twenty dyed.

  5. Why does the page need a second, dilute movie of the same particles to measure S(q)?Discuss

    Model answer

    The amplitude A(q) is the product of what one particle looks like through the microscope (its size, the dye, the optics, the camera) and how the crowd arranges, S(q). In a dilute enough movie the particles hardly feel each other and S is close to 1, so dividing the amplitudes per particle cancels everything but S(q). A real reference is never perfectly structureless: at 4 % hard spheres already have S(0) of about 0.7 (Carnahan-Starling: (1 - φ)4 / (1 + 4φ + 4φ2 - 4φ3 + φ4) = 0.73 at φ = 0.04), so dividing by it would make S(q) of the crowded sample read about 40 % too high at the widest patterns (1 / 0.73 = 1.37), as the page warns. The reference must be much more dilute, or its own S(q) corrected for. On the page the reference particles do not interact, by construction, and S = 1 exactly. The same trick, dividing the rates, cancels most of what the depth in focus adds to D(q).

Proteins

Page: Do they attract or repel? The antibody has a molar mass of 148 000 g/mol and a hydrodynamic radius of 5.3 nm.

  1. What is the diffusion coefficient of one antibody alone in water at 22 °C? How fast must the camera be to follow a pattern of q = 2 µm-1?Compute

    Model answer

    D0 = 4.07 × 10-21 / (6π × 0.955 × 10-3 × 5.3 × 10-9) = 42.7 µm2/s. At q = 2 µm-1 the pattern fades in 1/(D0q2) = 5.9 ms: the camera needs a few hundred frames per second, 500 on the page, and an exposure shorter than that time.

    Assumes: one antibody alone, a sphere of Rh = 5.3 nm, water at 22 °C.

  2. The hydrodynamic volume per gram is vh = (4/3)πRh3NA/M. Compute it, then kD = 1.45 vh and A2 = 4vh/M for antibodies that were plain hard spheres.Compute

    Model answer

    vh = 4.19 × (5.3 × 10-7 cm)3 × 6.022 × 1023 / 148 000 = 2.54 mL/g. Hard spheres: kD = 3.7 mL/g and A2 = 6.9 × 10-5 mol mL/g2. Both positive: spheres that only exclude each other keep apart and spread faster when crowded.

    Assumes: the antibody as a hard sphere of its hydrodynamic radius, to first order in concentration.

  3. At 115 mM salt the page's antibody has A2 = 4.0 × 10-5 mol mL/g2. Use kD = 2A2M - 6.55 vh to find kD. How can A2 be positive and kD negative?Compute

    Model answer

    kD = 2 × 4.0 × 10-5 × 148 000 - 6.55 × 2.54 = 11.8 - 16.6 = -4.8 mL/g (the page gives -4.9). A2 is the balance of the forces alone: positive, the antibodies still keep apart on balance, mostly by their size. kD also holds the drag each protein exerts on its neighbours through the liquid, the 6.55 vh, which slows the crowd down. When the attraction has eaten up part of the repulsion, the drag wins and kD turns negative.

    Assumes: the first-order relation between kD and A2 with the hard-sphere hydrodynamic term, as on the page.

  4. With kD = -4.9 mL/g, what diffusion coefficient does the movie give at 30 mg/mL? Read as the motion of one protein alone, what radius would that suggest?Compute

    Model answer

    D = D0(1 + kDc) = 42.7 × (1 - 4.9 × 0.030) = 36.4 µm2/s, with c in g/mL. Read as one protein, R = 5.3 nm × 42.7 / 36.4 = 6.2 nm, 17 % too large. Only the line through several concentrations, extrapolated to zero, gives the radius of one antibody on its own.

    Assumes: D linear in concentration up to 30 mg/mL.

  5. The amplitude per gram of protein falls with the concentration as S(0) = 1 / (1 + c/c0), with c0 = 1/(2A2M). For A2 = 4.0 × 10-5 mol mL/g2, what is c0, and by how much does the amplitude per gram fall at 30 mg/mL?Compute

    Model answer

    c0 = 1 / (2 × 4.0 × 10-5 × 148 000) = 0.084 g/mL = 84 mg/mL. At 30 mg/mL, S(0) = 1 / (1 + 30/84) = 0.74: the amplitude per gram is 26 % below that of a dilute solution. That bend of <A> against c is how the page measures A2, the static virial coefficient, from the same movies.

    Assumes: the virial form S(0) = 1/(1 + c/c0), adequate at these concentrations.

  6. In a buffer with little salt the antibodies repel each other and kD is about +14 mL/g. The salt goes from 15 to 115 mM. Does kD go up, down, or stay?Predict

    Model answer

    Down, to about -5 mL/g. The salt ions screen the charge of each antibody, so the repulsion fades within a short distance (the Debye length falls from 2.5 to 0.9 nm). What is left, the drag of neighbours and a short-range attraction, makes diffusion slow down as the concentration rises.

    Try it on the page: 115 mM salt.

Sheared materials

Page: When does it start to flow? The plates are 500 µm apart and the shear runs at 1 Hz.

  1. The strain amplitude is 50 %. How far does the top plate move each way? A bead 10 µm above the focal plane: how much further than the focal plane does it move?Compute

    Model answer

    The strain is displacement over gap: 0.5 × 500 µm = 250 µm each way. Each layer moves in proportion to its height, so the bead 10 µm above focus moves 0.5 × 10 = 5 µm more than the focal plane: within a cycle, beads at different depths slide past each other by several micrometres, and patterns across the flow are scrambled.

    Assumes: a linear velocity profile across the gap and no slip at the plates.

  2. Below its yield point the microgel rearranges with D = 2.2 × 10-4 µm2/s along the flow. How far does a bead wander per cycle, along that direction? How many cycles does it take for the echo of a pattern with q = 5 µm-1 to fall to 1/e?Compute

    Model answer

    Per cycle (1 s), √(2DΔt) = √(2 × 2.2 × 10-4) = 0.021 µm, about 20 nm: far less than a pixel. The echo falls to 1/e after 1/(Dq2) = 1/(2.2 × 10-4 × 25) = 180 s, so about 180 cycles: this is why echo-DDM needs long movies of many cycles, and why it can see motions far below the resolution of the microscope.

    Assumes: diffusive rearrangements at a steady rate, frames compared at zero strain.

  3. A rubber is sheared at 50 %. Within a cycle the patterns across the flow fade. How much of them is left exactly one cycle later?Predict

    Model answer

    All of it. An elastic solid deforms with the plates and springs back: every bead is back where it was at each full cycle, so the echoes reach 1. A real rubber in a real cell shows the floor of the method: what the cell and the camera add on their own.

    Try it on the page: the rubber.

  4. 2 µm beads are in a glycerol-water mix of 0.19 Pa s at 22 °C. What is their Brownian diffusion coefficient D0? The liquid is sheared at 100 % strain and the echoes are compared at zero strain: what D do they show along the vorticity, and along the flow?Compute

    Model answer

    D0 = kBT / (6πηa) = 4.07 × 10-21 / (6π × 0.19 × 10-6) = 1.1 × 10-15 m2/s = 1.1 × 10-3 µm2/s. Along the vorticity the echoes show D0: the shear does not act in that direction. Along the flow they show more. The flow of the liquid undoes itself, but the bead also diffuses: a step dh up or down while the strain is γ carries it along the flow by γ dh, and the reverse half of the cycle does not bring it back. Over whole cycles this adds γ02D0/2 (the strain squared averaged over a cycle is γ02/2), so D = D0(1 + 1/2) = 1.7 × 10-3 µm2/s: shear-induced dispersion, real motion of the beads.

    Assumes: sinusoidal strain, height motion not limited by the walls over a few cycles, frames compared at zero strain over whole cycles, no slip or secondary flows.

    Try it on the page: the viscous liquid.

  5. In the simulated microgel, rearrangements move a bead within the material, whose own coordinates are back in place whenever the strain is zero. A bead wanders along the height between two echoes as well as along the flow. If the echoes are compared at a phase where the strain is γ, the bead is off along the flow by γ Δh. Averaged over the cycle, this adds γ02 Dh / 2 to the apparent D along the flow. By how much is D along the flow overestimated at 150 % strain, if Dh equals the true D along the flow?Compute

    Model answer

    γ02/2 = 1.52/2 = 1.125: the apparent D is 2.1 times the true one. At 20 % strain the extra term is only 2 %. Comparing frames that start at zero strain, as the page does, removes it, but needs the camera to know the phase of the shear.

    Assumes: the rearrangements happen in the material's own coordinates, as in the simulated microgel. For a bead diffusing in a liquid (7.4), part of the extra fading is real dispersion and stays at zero strain.

Moving tissues

Page: Jammed, flowing or flocking? The cells are 15 µm across.

  1. The ring of strongest patterns sits at q = 0.48 µm-1. What spacing is that, and why is it the strongest?Compute

    Model answer

    2π/q = 13 µm, a little less than the size of a cell: the spacing of neighbouring nuclei. The sheet is packed, so the nuclei sit at nearly regular distances, and that pattern is reinforced by the arrangement (the peak of the structure factor), as in a crowd of particles.

    Assumes: the strongest patterns at the spacing of neighbours, as in a packed sheet.

  2. The displacement is read from f as a Gaussian, <Δr2> = −4 ln f / q2, while 0.1 < f < 0.95. At q = 0.62 µm-1, what is the largest and the smallest displacement it can measure?Compute

    Model answer

    Largest, at f = 0.1: 4 × 2.30 / 0.38 = 24 µm2, an rms displacement of about 5 µm, a third of a cell. Smallest, at f = 0.95: 4 × 0.051 / 0.38 = 0.53 µm2, about 0.7 µm. Displacements beyond the reach need wider patterns, where the arrangement of the cells muddles the reading.

    Assumes: the Gaussian reading of f, valid where the displacements are close to Gaussian.

  3. A jammed sheet: cells crawl at 6 µm/h but relative to their neighbours move 2 µm in ten hours. A fluid sheet: cells crawl at 30 µm/h and, relative to their neighbours, diffuse with an effective D of about 70 µm2/h. How long does a cell in the fluid sheet take to move one cell size?Compute

    Model answer

    In two dimensions <Δr2> = 4Dt, so one cell size, 225 µm2, takes 225 / 280 = 0.8 h, under an hour. In the jammed sheet, at the pace of 2 µm in ten hours, it would take far longer than the cells live without dividing: over the times a movie covers, they are caged.

    Assumes: diffusive motion relative to the sheet at the effective D given; "caged" means over the times this movie covers.

  4. A sheet moves as one block at 7 µm/h and its cells keep their neighbours. Seen in the frame of the microscope, after 2 hours, what mean squared displacement would the cells show? Is the sheet solid or liquid?Predict

    Model answer

    (7 × 2)2 = 196 µm2, nearly a cell area: in the frame of the microscope it looks fluid. It is a flocking solid: relative to the sheet the cells hardly move. Jammed or fluid is about how cells move relative to their neighbours, so the motion of the whole sheet must be taken out first.

    Try it on the page: a flocking solid.

  5. Each cell turns towards the direction it is pushed in. Why does this make a jammed sheet move as a block instead of melting it?Discuss

    Model answer

    Neighbours push each other, so they soon steer the same way; once all cells crawl in one direction, nobody pushes against anybody, and the cages stay intact while the whole sheet travels. Alignment turns crawling against each other into crawling together: it promotes collective motion and, at low speed, solidification, as Giavazzi et al. (2018) found in their model. Faster cells still squeeze past each other while travelling: a flocking liquid.

  6. In the network of nuclei (step 8) a cell of the fluid sheet keeps 70 % of its neighbours after one hour; one of the jammed sheet keeps 99.8 %. A cell has six neighbours. About how many has it swapped in an hour, in each sheet? And how does the first number fit with question 8.3?Compute

    Model answer

    Fluid: 0.3 × 6 = 1.8, about two neighbours an hour. Jammed: 0.002 × 6 = 0.01, one swap in about a hundred hours. In question 8.3 a fluid cell moves one cell size in under an hour, which is what it takes to slip between two neighbours: so a swap or two per hour is the right order. The neighbours kept is a measure of the same rearrangements that DDM sees as the displacement relative to the sheet, read cell by cell instead of from the patterns.

    Assumes: six neighbours per cell and swaps that do not undo each other within the hour.

    Try it on the page: the fluid sheet.

  7. Stain the nuclei and the cell edges with the same dye and film them together. The strongest stripe patterns of each stain alone are at the spacing of neighbours, yet together that pattern is about 25 times weaker. Why? What does it mean for DDM?Discuss

    Model answer

    An edge lies half way between two neighbouring nuclei, so at the stripe spacing of neighbours the edges are bright where the nuclei are dark: the two patterns are out of step by half a period and cancel when added. DDM then gets little signal at the spacing of neighbours, but that is not where it reads the displacement anyway (the arrangement of the cells slows those patterns down); a little finer, where nuclei and edges no longer cancel, it works as before. The page looks for the spacing among patterns 0.6 to 2 cells wide, so that the much wider patterns, now the strongest, are not mistaken for it.

  8. In the fluid sheet (step 6, stretched or compressed exponential) the rate is 4.5 per hour at q = 0.64 µm-1 and 7.9 per hour at q = 1.15 µm-1, with β near 1.75. How does the rate grow with q? If each cell went straight with a velocity drawn from an isotropic Gaussian distribution (each component Gaussian; in two dimensions the speeds then follow a Rayleigh distribution), f = exp(−q2v2Δt2/4) with v the rms speed. What speed do the two rates give?Compute

    Model answer

    7.9 / 4.5 = 1.76 and 1.15 / 0.64 = 1.80: the rate grows about as q, the sign of motion in straight lines over these distances (diffusion would give 1.82 = 3.2). The model falls to 1/e when qvΔt/2 = 1, so Γ = qv/2 and v = 2Γ/q = 2 × 4.5 / 0.64 = 14 µm/h, and 2 × 7.9 / 1.15 = 13.7 µm/h, nearly the same. The simulated cells move at 14 µm/h (rms, relative to the sheet, over one frame): slower than the 30 µm/h they would crawl on their own, because their neighbours push back. The compressed shape (β between 1 and 2) says the motion is not quite straight: a pure Gaussian spread of straight paths would give β = 2, so the relation Γ = qv/2 is an idealised comparison, not an exact reading.

    Assumes: straight paths with an isotropic Gaussian velocity distribution; motion relative to the sheet.
