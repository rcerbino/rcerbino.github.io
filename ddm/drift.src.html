The simulations are simple (for example, the particles do not interact) and rely on many approximations. Found something wrong or not working? Particles carried by a flow, by gravity or by heat

Particles in a liquid jiggle at random, but they can also be carried in one direction: by a flow of the liquid, by gravity when they are denser than the liquid, or by a temperature gradient, which pushes most colloids towards the cold side (thermophoresis). Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) sees both motions in the same movie. The random part makes each stripe pattern fade; the drift makes it travel, so patterns along the drift fade and oscillate at the same time. Here a simulated microscope lets you measure the speed and the direction of the drift, and find out when it is too slow to be seen at all.

Hands on

The bench

Choose what carries the particles, then the sample, the microscope and the camera (controls on the left, or just below on a phone). The movie is recorded again and every step below updates. "Copy a link to this setup" gives an address that opens the bench with the same settings.

1

Set up the microscope

The microscope sees a round field 25 mm across in its image plane. A camera chip covers a rectangle in it, and you choose which part of the chip to read out. For a drift the size of the field matters more than usual: the widest stripe pattern the image holds is what shows a slow drift first.

A microscope usually stands upright, with its optical axis vertical. Gravity then pulls the particles along the axis, where DDM does not look. Laid on its side, with the axis horizontal, gravity points down the image.

How one particle looks

The math: how each image is computed

As on the sizing page: the light scattered by each sphere (Mie theory) interferes with the transmitted light through the condenser and objective apertures (bright field, phase contrast, dark field, DIC), or the dyed sphere is convolved with the point spread function (fluorescence and confocal). Each frame is the sum of the images of all particles at their positions, with the detector's pixel size, quantum efficiency, shot noise, read noise and full well.

Motion. Each particle diffuses in 3D with the Stokes-Einstein coefficient D = kBT / (3πηd) and is carried by a drift v: in the image plane (a flow, sedimentation with the microscope on its side, a temperature gradient across the image) or along the axis (sedimentation with an upright microscope, a gradient along the axis, with reflecting walls). A flow pushed through the chamber has the Poiseuille profile v(h) = 6 v̄ h(H − h) / H2, with v̄ its mean over the chamber of thickness H. The particles do not interact, and they do not change the flow.

2

Record a movie

Particles that leave the image on one side come back on the other: the simulated field repeats like a tile, as the Fourier transform assumes.

In fastDDM

The structure function below is the same as fastDDM's. With your own movie you would start from the image files:

import fastddm as fd
img_seq = fd.read_images(file_names)
3

Subtract two frames

Subtract a frame from one taken a little later. Everything that stays still cancels out; what is left is what moved, plus the camera noise.

The math: the difference image
ΔI(x; t, Δt) = I(x, t + Δt) − I(x, t)
4

Split it into stripe patterns

Any image can be built by adding up stripe patterns of different spacing and direction. The Fourier transform tells how much of each pattern the difference image contains, averaged over the whole movie. The centre of the map holds wide stripes, the edge fine ones.

Without a drift the map is round: the particles wander equally in all directions. A drift makes the patterns along it travel. After the delay, a pattern that moved by exactly one stripe looks as before and almost cancels in the difference; one that moved by half a stripe is reversed and shows twice as strongly. So the map is crossed by dark and bright fringes at right angles to the drift, closer together for a faster drift or a longer delay. Move the delay slider and watch them close in.

The second map shows the same as a share: red where the pattern is still there, white where it has faded, blue where it came back reversed. Only a drift can turn a pattern around.

The difference image cannot tell a drift to the right from one to the left: the fringes are the same for both. The phase of each pattern can. Comparing the Fourier transform of a frame with that of a later frame, each pattern has moved along by a phase, and the phase map (colour wheel) shows it: the colours run along the drift and turn faster for finer stripes. Reverse the flow and the colours run the other way. Faint colours: patterns already faded.

Predict first: the flow is reversed, from left to right to right to left. Does the strength map (top left) change?
No. The difference only keeps how far each pattern travelled, not which way, so the fringes stay where they are. The phase map (Student level) turns its colours around. The bench now runs with the flow reversed.
The math: structure function and cross spectrum with a drift

A particle carried by a drift v while it diffuses multiplies the Fourier transform of the image by e−iq·vΔt as well as letting it fade. For independent particles

d(q, Δt) = ⟨ |F[ΔI](q; t, Δt)|2 ⟩t = A(q) [1 − e−Dq2Δt cos(q·vΔt)] + B
C(q, Δt) = ⟨ F(q, t + Δt) F*(q, t) ⟩t = (A(q)/2) e−Dq2Δt e−iq·vΔt

The fringes in d are the lines cos(q·vΔt) = 1, spaced 2π / (vΔt) along v. The normalised map is f = 1 − (d − B) / A = e−Dq2Δt cos(q·vΔt), between −1 and 1. The cosine is even in v, so d gives the speed and the line of the drift but not its sign. The phase of C is −q·vΔt, sign included; the camera noise, uncorrelated between frames, drops out of C at Δt > 0. The phase map shows arg C, with its brightness set by |C| / (A/2). Map axes: qx to the right, qy up.

dqt = fd.ddm(img_seq, range(1, len(img_seq) // 2))
dqt.pixel_size = pixel_size        # um
dqt.set_frame_rate(frame_rate)     # frames per second
# dqt.data[k] is d(q, dt) on the full (qy, qx) plane: with a drift, keep
# it 2D (or average in sectors of direction) instead of along circles
5

Watch each pattern change, along and across the drift

For each stripe spacing, follow how much of the pattern is left as the delay grows. Patterns across the drift (stripes parallel to it) are not moved by it: they only fade, as without a drift. Patterns along the drift fade at the same rate but also travel, so the curves swing below zero and back before they die out. Fine stripes swing sooner, because the drift needs less time to carry them by one spacing; but they also fade sooner, and may be gone before they swing.

The curves are f(q, Δt) = 1 − (d − B) / A averaged over the directions of q within 15° of the drift (left) or of the direction across it (right). The lines are the model e−Dq2Δt cos(q·vΔt), with the D and v of the result, averaged over the same directions: across the drift q·v is small but not zero, so at long delays even those curves dip below zero when the drift is fast.

6

From the curves to the drift

Each pattern gives two numbers: how fast it fades, and how fast it swings along the drift. Fading comes from the random motion: halving the stripe spacing makes it four times faster, a line of slope 2 on these logarithmic axes. Swinging comes from the drift: halving the spacing makes it only twice as fast, a line of slope 1. The height of the first line gives the diffusion coefficient, and from it the size; the height of the second gives the speed.

The two lines cross. To their left the drift moves each pattern before diffusion renews it, and the drift can be seen. To their right diffusion wins. If the crossing lies at wider stripes than the image can hold, or the movie is too short for the drift to move the widest pattern by a good part of its spacing, the drift is invisible to DDM however much you look: the result then says so.

Below the plot, the result gives the speed, its direction and the diffusion coefficient, and what they say about the cause: the density of the particles for sedimentation, the thermal diffusion coefficient and the Soret coefficient for thermophoresis.

Predict first: you double the flow speed. Do the dots (the fading rates) move?
No. Fading comes from diffusion alone, which does not care about the flow: the dots stay on the same line, and the size does not change. The squares (swinging) move up by a factor two, and the crossing of the two lines moves to finer stripes, so the drift shows in more patterns. The bench now runs with twice the speed.
The math: fading, swinging, and when a drift can be seen
Γ(q) = D q2,    ω(q) = q v (along v)   ⇒   dh = kBT / (3πηD)

The two rates are equal at q* = v/D. A pattern along the drift swings visibly when qvΔt reaches about 1 before diffusion has erased it, Dq2Δt of about 2: that is for q below 2v/D, and above 1/(vΔtmax) for the longest delay of the movie. The widest pattern of the image has q = 2π/L, so a drift shows only if the Peclet number over the field, v L/D, is above about 2π. The page asks for at least three measured q in that range and a speed more than three times its uncertainty.

Velocity. The phase of C(q, Δt) is fitted by weighted least squares to −q·vΔt over all pixels and delays where the pattern is still clearly there (|C| above a tenth of A) and the phase has not wrapped (|q·vΔt| < 1.2), each weighted by |C|2; a first guess comes from the phase difference between neighbouring pixels, which does not wrap. The uncertainty combines two jackknifes (root mean square), the fit repeated with one part left out each time: over ten parts of the movie (noise from frame to frame) and over eight interleaved groups of q (the arrangement of the particles, which the widest patterns keep through the whole movie, so parts of one movie share it). D then comes from a fit of d over 12 sectors of direction with v fixed, A free at each q and B from the camera. The fits of Γ and ω at each q (the plot) are for the eye and for the scatter of D.

Sedimentation. Stokes: vs = (2/9) Δρ g a2 / η, with a the radius. From the measured vs and D (which gives a), Δρ = 9ηvs / (2g a2). Thermophoresis. v = −DT ∇T, and the Soret coefficient is ST = DT/D; positive ST sends the particles to the cold side. For polystyrene beads ST grows with temperature and changes sign near 5-15 °C (Iacopini, Rusconi and Piazza 2006; Braibanti, Vigolo and Piazza 2008). In a gradient along the axis the particles pile up against one wall within 1/(ST |∇T|).

What this page adds to the published methods

    
The analysis of drift in DDM usually fits the structure function with its cos(q·vΔt) term (see "Go further"). The steps below are not in the papers we cite: we added them while testing the page against its own simulations. Some may exist elsewhere in the literature. At the "Addict" level each can be switched off, and the result table shows the published method alone on the same movie.

    
    
      
  • Velocity from the phase of the cross spectrum. The phase of ⟨F(q, t + Δt) F*(q, t)⟩ is −q·vΔt: it gives the sign of the drift, needs no model of the fading, and has no camera noise at Δt > 0. Step 6. For 200 nm beads at 2 µm/s the structure function alone gave 2.9 µm/s, and 1.4 µm/s for a sample with no drift at all; the phase gave 1.93 ± 0.09 and no drift.
    • 
      
  • Camera noise known from the detector. B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise2), the same at every q, instead of a free B at each q. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Uncertainty from the movie itself. The velocity fit repeated with one tenth of the movie left out, and with one of eight interleaved groups of q left out (jackknifes), combined. Step 6. Parts of the movie alone gave uncertainties up to three times too small, because the widest patterns keep the same particles through the whole movie.
    • 
      
  • Can the drift be seen? The range of q where the drift moves the patterns before diffusion renews them, and the Peclet number over the field, said in words. Step 6.

About this tool

    
This page is a simple tool, generated with Claude Opus 5.5, that simulates a realistic microscopy experiment in which DDM is used to study colloidal particles that diffuse and drift. The microscope, light sources, cameras, spinning disk and point scanner are modelled mostly on the instruments of our SoMeX lab. The particles are spheres that do not interact with each other, so every sample is a dilute suspension; the thermophoretic values come from the measurements cited on the page. The aim is not to produce research-quality data, but to give anyone thinking of using DDM an idea of what a drift does to a DDM experiment, and of when it can be measured.

    
If you find something that looks wrong or does not work, please let us know through the contact page.
