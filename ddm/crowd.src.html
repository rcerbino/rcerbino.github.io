The simulations are simple (for example, the flow of the liquid between the particles enters only through theory) and rely on many approximations. Found something wrong or not working? Crowded particles: how they arrange, and how the crowd moves

Fill a liquid with particles and they start to get in each other's way. Each one sits in a cage of neighbours, and the crowd takes on an order: not the rows of a crystal, but a typical distance between neighbours. That order shows up as a peak in the structure factor S(q), and it changes how the crowd moves: density patterns at the spacing of neighbours fade slowly, wide ones fast, because the crowd pushes back when it is squeezed. Light scattering has measured this for decades. Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) does it with a confocal microscope, from the same movie, for structure and motion together (Lu et al. 2012). Here the particles of a simulated crowd push each other, and the page compares what DDM finds with what they really do.

Hands on

The bench

Choose how crowded the sample is and how the particles interact, then the microscope and the camera (controls on the left, or just below on a phone). The particles are simulated one small step at a time, pushing each other, so a run takes from ten seconds to a minute. "Copy a link to this setup" gives an address that opens the bench with the same settings.

1

Set up the microscope

The microscope sees a round field 25 mm across in its image plane. A camera chip covers a rectangle in it, and you choose which part of the chip to read out. In a crowd the particles above and below the focus would blur into a haze: a confocal microscope, whose pinholes keep only a thin layer in focus, sees the crowd one layer at a time.

How one particle looks

The math: how each image is computed, and how the particles move

As on the sizing page: each dyed particle is convolved with the point spread function of the microscope (fluorescence and confocal), or its scattered light interferes with the transmitted light (bright field and the other transmitted-light modes). Each frame is the sum of the images of the dyed particles at their positions, with the detector's pixel size, quantum efficiency, shot noise, read noise and full well. Undyed particles push like the others but do not show.

Motion. Brownian dynamics in a box that repeats sideways and in depth (no walls). At each time step h every particle gets a random kick of variance 2D0h along x, y and z, with D0 = kBT / 3πησ, plus the push of its neighbours; then any two spheres that overlap are moved apart along the line of their centres (Heyes and Melrose 1993). Charged spheres also repel with a screened Coulomb (Yukawa) potential,

U(r) / kBT = U0 σ e−κ(r − σ) / r,    r > σ

with 1/κ the Debye length. The free kicks are 3 % of the diameter, and the repulsion moves a particle by no more than that where it is 10 kBT. The particles start at random, are pushed apart, and wander for one Brownian time τB = σ2/D0 before the movie starts. The liquid between them is not simulated: no hydrodynamic interactions. The true S(q) shown below is computed from the positions of all particles at eight moments of the movie.

With the flow of the liquid (hard spheres, fluorescence and confocal). Following particles that push each other through the liquid takes a computation far beyond a browser, so the movie is then drawn from theory as a Gaussian field of density, in the same periodic box. Each Fourier mode Q = (k, kz) of the density fluctuates with variance N S(Q) (Percus-Yevick) and fades as exp[−D0Q2H(Q)Δt / S(Q)], the short-time rate, at all delays; H(Q) is the hydrodynamic function in the δγ scheme of Beenakker and Mazur (1984), as written by Genz and Klein (1991). With a share x dyed, the dyed density adds the fluctuation of the dye among the particles (variance N x(1 − x), rate DsQ2, with the short-time self-diffusion Ds of the same theory). The image of one particle at each depth (step 1) turns each mode into the image. Checked against particles that do not interact (S = H = 1): the same amplitudes within 2 %. Left out: blur during the exposure, the row timing of the scanner, and the slowing of the cages at long delays.

2

Record a movie

A second, short movie of the same particles, dilute, is recorded with the same microscope and camera: it gives how strong the pattern of one particle alone is, against which the crowd is measured. It is not shown.

In fastDDM

The structure function below is the same as fastDDM's. With your own movies (the sample and the dilute reference) you would start from the image files:

import fastddm as fd
img_seq = fd.read_images(file_names)
ref_seq = fd.read_images(reference_file_names)
3

Subtract two frames

Subtract a frame from one taken a little later. Everything that stays still cancels out; what is left is what moved, plus the camera noise.

The math: the difference image
ΔI(x; t, Δt) = I(x, t + Δt) − I(x, t)
4

Split it into stripe patterns

Any image can be built by adding up stripe patterns of different spacing and direction. The Fourier transform tells how much of each pattern the difference image contains, averaged over the whole movie. The centre of the map holds wide stripes, the edge fine ones.

In a crowd each pattern is a wave of density: more particles along the bright stripes, fewer along the dark ones. A pattern whose spacing matches the distance between neighbours fits the crowd's own order, so it is strong; wide patterns would need many particles to bunch up, which the crowd resists, so they are weak. A ring at the spacing of neighbours appears on the map.

The second map shows how much of each pattern is left after the delay: red where it is still all there, the colour of the page where it has faded.

The dashed circle on the strength map marks the finest pattern the objective can resolve.

The math: structure function of a crowd

For N identical dyed particles,

d(q, Δt) = ⟨ |F[ΔI](q; t, Δt)|2 ⟩ = A(q) [1 − f(q, Δt)] + B,    A(q) = 2N |K(q)|2 S(q)

with K the image of one particle (the optics of step 1), S(q) the structure factor, and f(q, Δt) = F(q, Δt) / S(q) the normalised collective intermediate scattering function, where F(q, Δt) = N−1 Σj,k ⟨exp{iq·[rj(Δt) − rk(0)]}⟩. With only a share x of the particles dyed, S becomes 1 + x(S − 1): with few dyed, only the terms j = k are left, and f follows single particles (self diffusion). Map axes: qx to the right, qy up.

dqt = fd.ddm(img_seq, lags)      # lags: the log-spaced delays
dqt.pixel_size = pixel_size      # um
aa = fd.azimuthal_average(dqt, bins=bins, range=(0, dqt.ky[-1]))
5

Watch each pattern change

For each stripe spacing, follow how much of the pattern is left as the delay grows. In a dilute sample fine stripes (red) fade first and wide ones (blue) last, each at the rate set by one particle diffusing. In a crowd the order is broken: the pattern at the spacing of neighbours fades more slowly than its neighbours on either side, because changing it means rearranging the cages.

The points are f(q, Δt) = 1 − (d − B) / A. The dashed lines are exp(−ΓΔt), with Γ the rate at which each pattern starts to fade, from the first delays (step 6).

Predict first: the volume fraction goes from 30 % to 45 %. Does the pattern at the spacing of neighbours fade faster or more slowly?
More slowly. A tighter crowd has a stronger order: the peak of S(q) grows, and the rate at the peak, D0 q² / S(q) at short times, drops with it. Each particle also has less room to move. The bench now runs at 45 %.
6

Structure and collective motion

How strong each pattern is, against the same particles far apart, is the structure factor S(q): the order of the crowd. It peaks at the spacing of neighbours, about 2π / q, and is low for wide patterns, because a crowd is hard to compress. The rate at which each pattern starts to fade, against one particle alone, gives the collective diffusion D(q): roughly D0 / S(q), slowest at the peak (de Gennes narrowing) and fastest for the widest patterns.

Both come out as ratios to the dilute reference movie: the optics, which make each particle's pattern weaker at fine stripes and add motion along the axis of the microscope, cancel out.

Predict first: same crowd, but only one particle in twenty is dyed. What happens to the peak of S(q)?
It disappears. The undyed particles still crowd the dyed ones, but the dyed ones are too far apart to show any order among themselves: S(q) becomes 1 + x (S - 1), nearly flat at x = 0.05. The patterns now follow single particles, which diffuse more slowly than alone, held back by their cages. The bench now runs with one particle in twenty dyed.
The math: from the curves to S(q) and D(q)
S(q) = [A(q) / N] / [Aref(q) / Nref]
Γ(q) = −d ln f / dΔt at short times = D0 q2 H(q) / S(q),    D(q) = D0 Γ(q) / Γref(q)

Amplitudes. A(q) = 2⟨|F(q)|2⟩ − B, from the mean power of each pattern, with B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise2), the camera noise, the same at every q. The reference is a movie of the same particles at a volume fraction of 4 %, without interactions, through the same optics, long enough for its particles to wander through the whole depth of the box twice (the number near the focus sets the amplitude); Aref and its rates are smoothed over seven neighbouring q. Only q where both movies carry more signal than B are used. The uncertainty of S(q) is the scatter over five neighbouring q.

Rates. At each q, a straight line through ln f against the delay, weighted by the noise of f, from the first delay down to f = 0.6 (at least three delays); its slope is −Γ. The same line through the reference movie gives Γref = D0q2 plus what motion along the axis adds: particles leaving the layer in focus change the patterns too. Their ratio gives D(q). Uncertainties from a jackknife over ten parts of the movie. Without hydrodynamic interactions the hydrodynamic function H(q) is 1. For hard spheres, the Percus-Yevick structure factor (Wertheim 1963) is drawn for comparison.

What the page does not fix. Each pattern of the image collects particles over the depth in focus, so it mixes in wave vectors along the axis: at the widest patterns S(q) and D(q) take in some of their neighbours at larger q, and the peak looks a little lower and broader. The short-time rate needs delays before the cages are felt; the fit reaches a little beyond them.

What this page adds to the published methods

    
Lu et al. (2012) measured S(q) of concentrated colloids with confocal DDM, as the ratio of the amplitude to that of a dilute sample, and the collective dynamics from f(q, Δt). The steps below are not in the papers we cite: we added them while testing the page against its own simulations, and some may exist elsewhere. At the "Addict" level each can be switched off, and the result table shows the published method alone on the same movies (B from the first delays, rates from a line through f = 1, D(q) = Γ / q², the reference bin by bin). The reference itself is recorded long enough for its particles to wander through the whole depth of the sample twice: with a short one the peak of S(q) came out 5-7 % low in our tests.

    
    
      
  • Camera noise known from the detector. B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise²), the same at every q. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • A line that may miss f = 1. The straight line through ln f over the first delays has its own intercept, so a small error in A does not tilt it. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Rates against the reference. D(q) = D0 Γ / Γref, with the rate of the same pattern in the dilute movie, instead of Γ / q²: motion along the axis of the microscope cancels out. Step 6. Without it, the widest patterns of a sample at 5 % read D more than twice too large through the spinning disk in our tests.
    • 
      
  • Reference smoothed over q. The amplitude and the rates of the dilute reference change smoothly with q, so they are smoothed over seven neighbouring q bins. Step 6. Bin by bin, the noise of the reference made the peak of S(q) 3-9 % too high in our tests (three samples).

About this tool

    
This page is a simple tool, generated with Claude Opus 5.5, that simulates a realistic microscopy experiment in which DDM is used to measure the structure and the collective motion of a concentrated suspension. The microscope, light sources, cameras, spinning disk and point scanner are modelled mostly on the instruments of our SoMeX lab. The particles are identical spheres that push each other by touching or by a screened charge repulsion, in a small box that repeats in every direction. The liquid between them is not simulated; its flow (hydrodynamic interactions) can be added for hard spheres through theory, and the movie is then drawn as a field of density rather than particle by particle. The aim is not to produce research-quality data, but to give anyone thinking of using DDM an idea of what it can see in a crowd, and of what limits it.

    
If you find something that looks wrong or does not work, please let us know through the contact page.
