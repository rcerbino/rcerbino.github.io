Is it a liquid or a solid? Microrheology with tracer beads

Many soft materials are a bit of both: a polymer solution springs back if you poke it quickly but flows if you wait, and a gel that is just forming is barely solid. Microrheology measures it without shearing the sample: it mixes in a few tiny beads and watches how far the thermal kicks of the molecules push them in a given time. In a liquid the beads wander off; in a solid they rattle in place. From that motion follow the storage modulus G' (how elastic) and the loss modulus G'' (how viscous), over many decades of frequency, from a drop of sample. Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) reads the motion of the beads from an ordinary microscope movie, without following any of them (Edera et al. 2017). Here a simulated microscope lets you do it on materials whose moduli are known exactly.

Hands on

The bench

Choose the material and the beads, then the microscope and the camera (controls on the left, or just below on a phone). The movie is recorded again and every step below updates. "Copy a link to this setup" gives an address that opens the bench with the same settings.

1

Set up the microscope

The microscope sees a round field 25 mm across in its image plane. A camera chip covers a rectangle in it, and you choose which part of the chip to read out. For microrheology a fast camera matters: the beads move only a little, and the fastest relaxations of the material are over in milliseconds. A small region of interest reads out faster.

How one bead looks

What this step gives: the finest stripes the movie can hold, and how strongly each particle shows in them: the objective and the light decide both.

The math: how each image is computed, and how the beads move

As on the sizing page: the light scattered by each bead (Mie theory) interferes with the transmitted light through the condenser and objective apertures, or the dyed bead is convolved with the point spread function (fluorescence and confocal). Each frame is the sum of the images of all beads at their positions, with the detector's pixel size, quantum efficiency, shot noise, read noise and full well. The material itself does not show.

Motion. The material is a solvent of viscosity ηs, Maxwell modes (modulus Gk, relaxation time τk) and, for a solid, an equilibrium modulus G∞:

G'(ω) = G∞ + Σ Gk (ωτk)2 / [1 + (ωτk)2],    G''(ω) = ηsω + Σ Gk ωτk / [1 + (ωτk)2]

A bead of radius a feels each mode as a spring 6πaGk in series with a dashpot 6πaGkτk, the plateau as a spring to its resting place and the solvent as a dashpot, each dashpot with its thermal noise. This is a linear (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) process, simulated exactly at every frame, along x, y and z alike; its mean squared displacement obeys the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation exactly. The beads do not interact, and the material is the same everywhere: no mesh that small beads slip through, no beads that stick.

2

Record a movie

The frames are log-spaced: in each cycle the first ones follow at the frame rate, then the gaps grow, and the cycle repeats. The same 512 frames then hold delays from milliseconds to about a minute, many decades of frequency in one movie.

What this step gives: a stack of frames and the time of each. The time between frames and the length of the movie set the fastest and the slowest changes that can be measured.

In fastDDM

The structure function below is the same as fastDDM's. With your own movie you would start from the image files:

import fastddm as fd
img_seq = fd.read_images(file_names)
3

Subtract two frames

Subtract a frame from one taken a little later. Everything that stays still cancels out; what is left is what moved, plus the camera noise.

What this step gives: for every pair of frames a delay apart, an image of what moved; what stayed put is gone. The longer the delay, the more has changed.

The math: the difference image
ΔI(x; t, Δt) = I(x, t + Δt) − I(x, t)
4

Split it into stripe patterns

Any image can be built by adding up stripe patterns of different spacing and direction. The Fourier transform tells how much of each pattern the difference image contains, averaged over the whole movie. The centre of the map holds wide stripes, the edge fine ones. The beads move the same way in every direction, so the map is round.

The charts further down label each pattern by q, a number that grows as the stripes get finer: q = 2π divided by the stripe spacing, in inverse micrometres. Stripes 1 µm apart have q of about 6.

A bead that moves by a fraction of a stripe spacing already changes that pattern; to renew a wide pattern it must go much further. So fine stripes are a magnifying glass for small motions, wide stripes for large ones, and together they cover displacements from nanometres to micrometres.

The second map shows how much of each pattern is left after the delay: red where it is still all there, the colour of the page where it has faded. In a gel the wide patterns stay red at every delay.

The dashed circle on the strength map marks the finest pattern the objective can resolve.

What this step gives: for each stripe spacing (each q, one ring of the map) and each delay, how much the movie has changed. A point near the centre of the map is a wide pattern, a point far out a fine one.

The math: structure function of tracer beads

For beads that do not interact, with Gaussian displacements,

d(q, Δt) = ⟨ |F[ΔI](q; t, Δt)|2 ⟩ = A(q) [1 − f(q, Δt)] + B,    f(q, Δt) = exp[−q2 ⟨Δr2(Δt)⟩ / 4]

with ⟨Δr2⟩ the mean squared displacement in the image plane. For free diffusion ⟨Δr2⟩ = 4DΔt and f = e−Dq2Δt, as on the sizing page. Map axes: qx to the right, qy up.

fastDDM's fd.ddm(img_seq, lags) works on evenly spaced frames: its lags count frames. With log-spaced frames (the standard here; "Download the movie" and "Frame times" give both files), the frames are paired by their times instead. This routine does it as the page does; on an exported movie it gives the same delays and d(q, Δt), apart from a few pairs that fall exactly on the edge between two groups of delays:

import numpy as np, tifffile

frames = tifffile.imread("movie.tif").astype(np.float64)       # T x n x n, from "Download the movie"
t = np.loadtxt("movie-times.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1)[:, 1]   # time of each frame (s), from "Frame times"
pixel_um = 0.1625                                              # pixel size in the sample (um): see the movie, step 2

T, n, _ = frames.shape
F = np.fft.fft2(frames)                                        # one transform per frame
dt = np.diff(t).min()                                          # shortest interval between frames

# every pair of frames up to half the movie apart, grouped by its delay: one group per whole number of
# shortest intervals up to 10, then 12 groups per decade; at most about T pairs per group, spread over the movie
i, j = np.triu_indices(T, 1)
gap = t[j] - t[i]
keep = gap <= (t[-1] - t[0]) / 2 * (1 + 1e-12)
i, j, gap = i[keep], j[keep], gap[keep]
u = np.round(gap / dt, 6)                                      # delay in shortest intervals (rounded: no float noise at the group edges)
group = np.where(u < 10.5, np.round(u) - 1, 10 + np.floor(12 * np.log10(u / 10.5))).astype(int)
taus, d2d = [], []
for g in np.unique(group):
    sel = np.flatnonzero(group == g)
    sel = sel[:: int(np.ceil(len(sel) / T))]
    if len(sel) < 4:
        continue
    acc = np.zeros((n, n))
    for a, b in zip(i[sel], j[sel]):
        acc += np.abs(F[b] - F[a]) ** 2
    taus.append(gap[sel].mean())                               # the delay of this group (s)
    d2d.append(acc / len(sel) / n**2)                          # d(q, dt) on the Fourier map
taus = np.array(taus)

# azimuthal average: rings of whole wave numbers, central cross left out (as fastDDM's central_cross_mask)
k = np.fft.fftfreq(n) * n
kx, ky = np.meshgrid(k, k)
ring = np.rint(np.hypot(kx, ky)).astype(int)
ring[(kx == 0) | (ky == 0) | (ring > n // 2)] = 0
count = np.bincount(ring.ravel(), minlength=n // 2 + 1)
dq = np.array([np.bincount(ring.ravel(), m.ravel(), n // 2 + 1) / np.maximum(count, 1) for m in d2d])[:, 1:]
q = 2 * np.pi * np.arange(1, n // 2 + 1) / (n * pixel_um)     # um^-1

5

Watch each pattern change

For each stripe spacing, follow how much of the pattern is left as the delay grows. Fine stripes (red) change first, because a bead only has to move a little to shift them; wide ones (blue) change later. In a liquid every curve falls to zero in the end: given time, the beads wander off and every pattern is renewed.

The points are measured: how different two frames a delay apart look, with the camera noise taken away and divided by the full change, then turned around so that 1 means the whole pattern is still there and 0 that it is gone. The lines are drawn from how far the beads move, found in step 6 from these same points, so they show that one displacement describes all the curves; the comparison with the simulated material in step 6 shows whether it is right.

The points are f(q, Δt) = 1 − (d − B) / A. The lines are exp[−q2 ⟨Δr2⟩ / 4] with the mean squared displacement of step 6 (leaving out the small effect of the depth of field), the same for every q: if the beads' displacements are Gaussian, one displacement explains all the curves. The order of the analysis. d is measured first; B comes from the camera and A from the mean power of each pattern; the points follow. The displacement comes from these points in step 6, so the lines test only that one displacement describes all of them; the comparison with the simulated material tests the result.

Can these beads escape? Predict first: the sample becomes a soft gel, in which each bead sits in a cage. Do the curves still fall to zero at long delays?
No. Each bead only rattles in its cage, so part of every pattern never changes: the curves level off above zero, higher for wider stripes, which a small rattle hardly moves. The height of the plateau gives how far the beads rattle, and from it the elastic modulus of the gel. The bench now runs with a soft gel so you can check: this chart keeps one curve of the earlier run, dashed, next to the new one at the same stripe spacing, and step 6 keeps the earlier displacement. Look for where the thick line levels off while the dashed one falls to zero.

What this step gives: how much of each pattern is left over time. Curves that level off above zero mean beads that stay caged.

6

From the curves to the moduli

Each curve of step 5 gives, at each delay, how far the beads have moved: the mean squared displacement, the average of the squared distances they travel, in square micrometres (its square root is a typical distance). Fine stripes give the small displacements of short delays, wide stripes the large ones of long delays, and where they overlap they agree. Free diffusion makes the displacement grow in proportion to the delay (a slope of 1 on these axes); beads held in cages make it level off (a slope of 0).

A rule that goes back to Einstein turns the displacement into the moduli: the stiffer the material, the less the beads move, and the faster it relaxes, the sooner they escape. A delay Δt stands for a frequency of about 1/Δt.

The generalised Stokes-Einstein relation turns the displacement into the moduli: the stiffer the material, the less the beads move, and the faster it relaxes, the sooner they escape. A delay Δt stands for a frequency of about 1/Δt.

The numbers behind this chart
Predict first: the beads are twice as large. Do the moduli change?
No. Larger beads move less: their mean squared displacement halves. But the rule that turns displacement into moduli multiplies it by the size again, so the moduli, which belong to the material, stay the same. The bench now runs with beads twice as large (at most 3 µm).
What the analysis is told, and what it finds

Told (inputs): the bead radius and the temperature; the pixel size and the times of the frames; the read noise and the gain of the camera, for B; the optics of the microscope, for the depth-of-field correction.

Found from the movie: how much of each pattern is left over time and the mean squared displacement of the beads. The moduli follow from it with the bead radius and the temperature it was told.

In this simulation the analysis uses the same model of the optics that made the movie, so agreement here shows that the analysis recovers what was simulated under those assumptions; with a real microscope the optics are another input that can be wrong.

What this step gives: the mean squared displacement of the beads, measured. The moduli are an interpretation: they come from the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation, with the bead size and the temperature the analysis is told.

A question to think about: You double the bead size and the mean squared displacement halves. Which quantity should come out the same, and why?

The moduli G' and G''. They belong to the material, and the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation multiplies the displacement by the bead radius, which undoes the change. If they did not come out the same, the beads would be probing something else than the bulk material: a layer depleted of polymer around them, or a mesh as large as they are.

The math: from f to the moduli

Notation. ⟨Δr2⟩, the mean squared displacement, measured in the image plane (two directions); for motion in three dimensions it is 1.5 times larger, ⟨Δr2⟩3D. J(t), the creep compliance: how much the material deforms under a steady stress, per unit of stress (1/Pa); Ĵ(ω) its one-sided Fourier transform. a, the bead radius. ω, the angular frequency in rad/s; the ordinary frequency is ω / 2π, in Hz. In Mason's formulas Γ is the gamma function (not a rate, as on the other pages) and α the local slope of ⟨Δr2⟩ on logarithmic axes.

⟨Δr2(Δt)⟩ = −(4 / q2) ln f(q, Δt),    J(t) = πa ⟨Δr2(t)⟩3D / kBT,    ⟨Δr2⟩3D = 1.5 ⟨Δr2⟩

Amplitude and noise. A(q) = 2⟨|F(q)|2⟩ − B, from the mean power of each pattern, which holds the whole amplitude even when part of the pattern never changes during the movie; B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise2), the camera noise, the same at every q. The mean power counts everything in the image that stays put during the movie: in a gel the frozen positions of the beads, which belong in A, but also dust and uneven light, which do not. The difference images of step 3 are blind to those; the amplitude is not. The simulated movies have neither. In a real experiment, subtract the power of a movie taken the same way without beads, or, for a material that flows, use twice the variance over time of each pattern, which leaves out what never changes. Displacement. At each q and delay, ⟨Δr2⟩ is the value that gives the measured f, with the depth of field: beads moving up or down change their image too, which adds a factor Cq(⟨Δz2⟩) through the optics of step 1, with ⟨Δz2⟩ = ⟨Δr2⟩/2. Only points where f is clearly below 1 (by three times its noise, and by at least 1 %) and above 0.15 are used, from the fourth q bin up; the q are combined with weights from the noise of ln ⟨Δr2⟩, at least three per delay. The noise of f and the uncertainty of the result come from a jackknife over ten parts of the movie.

Moduli. G*(ω) = 1 / [iω Ĵ(ω)]. The measured J(t) is fitted with springs and dashpots side by side (Kelvin-Voigt elements, retardation times four per decade) and a free flow t/η, all with positive weights; the moduli of that sum follow exactly. A direct transform of the measured points (Evans et al. 2009) rang with their noise. For comparison (Researcher level) Mason's local power-law formulas, |G*(ω)| = kBT / [πa⟨Δr2(1/ω)⟩3D Γ(1 + α)], G' = |G*| cos(πα/2), G'' = |G*| sin(πα/2), with α the local slope of ⟨Δr2⟩ on logarithmic axes.

What this page adds to the published methods

    
DDM microrheology was introduced by Edera et al. (2017), and Bayles, Squires and Helgeson (2017) did the same independently: f(q, Δt) turned into a mean squared displacement at each q, then into the moduli through the generalised Stokes-Einstein relation. Edera et al. chose A and B by making the displacements from all q agree; in a simulation the camera is known, so this page gets B from the photon count and A from the mean power instead. The steps below are not in the papers we cite: we added them while testing the page against its own simulations, and some may exist elsewhere. At the "Addict" level each can be switched off, and the result table shows a plain method on the same movie (A from the variance, B from the first delays, no depth of field, q where the displacement is below 1/q², Mason's formulas).

    
    
      
  • Amplitude from the mean power. A = 2⟨|F(q)|²⟩ − B instead of 2 var − B. Step 6. When the widest patterns are not renewed within the movie the variance misses part of A: on the standard micelles this alone reads the viscosity three times too low (85 instead of 250 mPa s).
    • 
      
  • Camera noise known from the detector. B = 2 (excess-noise factor × mean count + read noise²), the same at every q. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Depth of field. Beads moving up and down change each pattern too; the inversion includes it through the optics of step 1, as on the sizing page. Step 6.
    • 
      
  • Points chosen by their noise. Each q is used where f is clearly below 1 and above 0.15, weighted by the noise of ln ⟨Δr²⟩ from a jackknife over ten parts of the movie; the widest patterns are left out. Step 6. The widest patterns read the displacement up to twice too large in our tests.
    • 
      
  • Moduli from a fitted retardation spectrum, positive by construction. Step 6. On the soft micelles Mason's formulas read G' near the crossover about 20 % off; the spectrum stays within a few percent.

About this tool

    
This page is a simple tool, generated with Claude Opus 5.5, that simulates a realistic microscopy experiment in which DDM is used to measure the moduli of a soft material from the motion of tracer beads. The microscope, light sources, cameras, spinning disk and point scanner are modelled mostly on the instruments of our SoMeX lab. The beads are spheres that do not interact with each other, in a material that is the same everywhere and whose moduli are set exactly; the concentrated micelles come from the measurements cited on the page, the other materials have values chosen for the page. The aim is not to produce research-quality data, but to give anyone thinking of using DDM an idea of what microrheology can measure, and of what limits it.

    
If you find something that looks wrong or does not work, please let us know through the contact page.
